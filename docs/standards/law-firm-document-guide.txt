Law Firm SOPs, Workflows and Playbooks
What a good one includes, what to leave out, how to organize it, who owns what, and how to measure whether it works.
1. The four document types
Firms get into trouble when they mix these up. A rigid SOP used where judgment is needed frustrates staff and clients. A flexible playbook used for trust accounting invites risk.
Type
 | What it is
 | Law firm examples
 | 
Policy
 | The rules and boundaries. Says what must and must not happen.
 | Trust accounting, confidentiality, AI use, file retention
 | 
Workflow
 | The map of how work moves through stages and between people.
 | Intake to signed engagement; matter open to matter close
 | 
SOP
 | Exact steps for one routine task. Same result every time.
 | Running a conflict check, opening a matter, depositing a retainer
 | 
Playbook
 | Guidance for situations that need judgment: preferred approach, fallbacks, limits, escalation.
 | Contract negotiation positions, an upset client, a fee dispute
 | 
How they fit: the policy sets the rules, the workflow shows the map, each box on the map gets an SOP, and the playbook covers the forks in the road.
2. What a good SOP includes and why
Section
 | What goes in it
 | Why it exists
 | 
Header
 | Title, ID number, version, owner (role), last reviewed, next review date
 | An outdated SOP is worse than none. This shows if it is current and who to ask.
 | 
Purpose
 | One or two sentences on what this task accomplishes
 | Tells the reader they are in the right document
 | 
Scope
 | When it applies and when it does not (practice area, matter type, jurisdiction)
 | Stops people from using it in the wrong situation
 | 
Trigger
 | The event that starts the task
 | People know exactly when to begin
 | 
Roles
 | Who does each part, linked to the RACI chart
 | Prevents gaps and double work
 | 
Before you start
 | Forms, system access and information needed
 | Avoids stalling halfway through
 | 
Steps
 | Numbered, one action per step, starting with a verb. Exact system names, menu paths and button labels. Screenshots where helpful.
 | The core of the document
 | 
Decision points and escalation
 | &quot;If X, do Y.&quot; Clear flags where the task stops and an attorney decides.
 | Keeps legal judgment with the lawyer and supports supervision duties for non-lawyer staff (ABA Model Rule 5.3)
 | 
Timing
 | Deadline or service level for the task (for example, conflict check within 1 business day)
 | Makes the task measurable
 | 
Quality check
 | What &quot;done right&quot; looks like and who reviews it
 | Catches errors before a client or court sees them
 | 
Done when
 | The finished output and where it is saved
 | Closes the loop
 | 
Related links
 | Templates, forms, policies, other SOPs
 | One source of truth. Link instead of copying.
 | 
Change log
 | Date, what changed, who approved
 | Audit trail for malpractice carriers, bar audits and training
 | 
Workflow document sections
Name and process owner: one role accountable for the whole flow
Start and end points: what kicks it off and what counts as finished
Stages in a swimlane chart: one lane per role so handoffs are visible
Handoffs: what passes between people, in which system, and how the next person is notified
Decision points: where the path splits
Target time per stage: where delays will be measured
Linked SOPs: one per box
KPIs: 3 to 5 measures for the flow
Playbook sections
Situations covered: when to open this playbook
Guiding principles: what the firm cares about most in these situations
Preferred approach: the standard position or first move
Acceptable fallbacks: options staff can use without asking
Hard limits: what nobody agrees to without attorney or partner approval
Escalation: who decides what, and how fast
Scripts and templates: sample language, emails, clauses
Examples: short fictional scenarios showing good calls
3. What to leave out
Real client names, matter facts or data. Use fictional examples. Confidentiality rules apply to internal documents too.
Legal judgment calls handed to non-lawyers. Mark these &quot;attorney decides&quot; instead.
Passwords and login details. These belong in a password manager.
Walls of text and backstory. Long SOPs get skimmed or skipped. If one runs past 2 to 3 pages, split it.
Vague words like &quot;as needed,&quot; &quot;if applicable,&quot; &quot;promptly&quot; or &quot;the usual way.&quot; Say exactly what and when.
Undefined jargon and internal nicknames. A new hire should be able to follow it on day one.
Copied content from other documents. Copies go stale. Link to the source.
Court rules or deadlines pasted in with no source or review date. These change. Link the rule and assign someone to check it.
People&apos;s names as owners. Use roles. People leave.
Several processes in one SOP. One task per SOP.
Policy mixed into steps. Keep the rule in the policy and link to it.
4. How to organize the library
Organize by client lifecycle, not by department
Staff look for documents by what they are doing, not by who owns them. Suggested sections:
Intake and conflicts
Engagement and onboarding
Matter management and calendaring
Documents, drafting and filing
Billing, trust and collections
Matter closing and file retention
Firm admin (HR, IT, marketing)
Use a numbering scheme
Prefix by section so documents are easy to reference: INT-01 Conflict Check, BIL-03 Trust Deposit.
Keep one home and one index
Store everything in one searchable place, ideally inside or linked from the practice management system
Keep a master index listing each document, its owner, status and next review date
Stack documents in order: policy, then workflow, then SOP, then checklist or template
5. Who handles what
Use a RACI chart for each workflow
R, Responsible: does the work
A, Accountable: owns the outcome and signs off. Only one per step.
C, Consulted: gives input before the step is done
I, Informed: told after it is done
Rules that matter in a law firm
Exactly one A per step. Two owners means no owner.
Any step involving legal judgment has an attorney as A
The supervising attorney is accountable for work delegated to paralegals and staff
Keep C&apos;s to a minimum. Every consult adds delay.
Assign roles, not names
Name a document owner for each SOP, separate from the process owner, whose job is keeping it current

6. Setting metrics and KPIs
How to choose them
Start with why the process exists (for example, intake exists to turn good leads into signed clients fast).
Name the outcome that proves it is working.
Pick one leading measure (predicts results, like response time) and one lagging measure (shows results, like conversion rate).
Measure a baseline for 30 to 60 days before changing anything.
Set a target, an owner and the exact report or field the number comes from.
Set a review rhythm: weekly for intake and deadlines, monthly for billing, quarterly for the rest.
Tests for a good KPI
It prompts a decision. If nobody would act on it, drop it.
It can be pulled from data the firm already logs. If the field is not filled in consistently, the number is wrong.
Keep 3 to 5 per workflow. More than that and nobody watches any of them.
Cover a mix: speed, quality, client experience, money, and whether the SOP is being followed
Examples by workflow
Workflow
 | Sample KPIs
 | 
Intake
 | Lead response time; consult-to-signed conversion rate; days from consult to signed engagement letter
 | 
Conflicts
 | Percent of checks done before the consult; turnaround time
 | 
Matter management
 | Case cycle time; time spent in each stage; missed or near-miss deadlines (target zero)
 | 
Documents
 | Rework rate (drafts sent back for errors); first-pass approval rate
 | 
Billing
 | Utilization, realization and collection rates; days from work to invoice; A/R over 90 days
 | 
Trust
 | Monthly three-way reconciliation done on time; number of exceptions
 | 
Client experience
 | Client satisfaction or NPS; response time to client messages; complaints
 | 
SOP health
 | Percent of documents reviewed on schedule; new hire time to working independently; errors traced to an unclear SOP
 | 
7. Keeping it current
Review high-risk documents (trust, deadlines, conflicts) quarterly and everything else yearly
Also review whenever software, staff roles or court rules change
Have the person who does the task write the first draft, then test it with someone who has never done it
Give staff an easy way to flag a step that is wrong
Train on each new or changed SOP and record who completed training
